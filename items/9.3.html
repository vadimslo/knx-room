Q1. In several paragraphs, provide a detailed overview of your token, your network/platform, and your token’s utility within the network/platform. Attach all relevant supporting documents, whitepaper(s), decks, or other materials, including any analysis or legal opinions you may currently have about the security law risks presented by your token.

Coinbase pre-launch questionnaire v2, question 1, 10 July 2026, revised 13 September 2026, token and network overview

Konnex is a dedicated Layer-1 blockchain for robotics and "Physical AI" - a permissionless marketplace where robots and autonomous systems negotiate, execute and settle real-world physical work. It is built in Rust on the Substrate framework as a custom, purpose-built Layer-1, optimised for decentralised AI and physical-work consensus.

AI "miners" supply robot-control and behaviour models; independent validators verify completed tasks via Proof-of-Physical-Work (PoPW), using deterministic 3D physics simulation before on-chain execution and settlement. Task settlement is stablecoin-native by design, while KNX - the network’s native coin - provides validator staking and security (with slashing), pays network/protocol fees, serves as AI-miner collateral, enables on-chain governance, supports a buyback flywheel, and is used for executor bonding.

Current status: pre-TGE. Two public testnets are live - Konnex: Ingest (testnet.konnex.world) and a functional subnet testnet (https://subnets.testnet.konnex.world/explorer), live since 25 April 2026. The runtime passed a SlowMist security audit in April 2026.

Supporting materials (available on request): Whitepaper - https://konnex.world/wp; website - https://konnex.world; token specification; token distribution plan; pitch deck; SlowMist audit report. Legal opinion: Chung Ting Fai & Co (Singapore), dated 3 February 2026, concludes that KNX is unlikely to be a security token / capital-markets product under the Singapore Securities and Futures Act. Note: that opinion was addressed to the project’s predecessor entity, prior to the current Dalithium Foundation (Panama) / Dalithium Inc (BVI) structure, and is provided for reference.

Q39. Please explain how transaction finality is achieved, whether probabilistically or deterministically. Please link any relevant documentation.

Coinbase pre-launch questionnaire v2, question 37, 10 July 2026, revised 13 September 2026, consensus and validators

Transaction finality on Konnex is deterministic, not probabilistic.

Block production uses Aura (round-robin block authors). Finality is provided by GRANDPA (GHOST-based Recursive ANcestor Deriving Prefix Agreement), the standard Substrate/Polkadot finality gadget: GRANDPA validators vote to finalize blocks, and once a block is GRANDPA-finalized it is considered irreversible under normal network operation.

Typical flow: a transaction is included in an Aura-produced block (~12s target block time), then GRANDPA finalizes that block and its ancestry. All on-chain state changes (transfers, subnet registrations, staking, etc.) inherit this L1 finality.

Relevant documentation:

  • Substrate/Polkadot consensus & GRANDPA: https://wiki.polkadot.network/docs/learn-consensus

Q73. Is the escrow structure non-custodial?

Upbit listing review, round two, 12 August 2026, settlement and custody

Fully non-custodial. The payee address, the amount and the deadline are fixed when the payer funds the escrow and cannot be changed by anyone afterwards. Only three operations exist: fund, release on a strict majority of independent resolvers, and refund after the deadline. The only two possible outcomes are release to the payee fixed at funding or refund to the payer; resolvers cannot redirect funds to another address, alter the amount, or take the funds themselves. If resolvers deadlock or never act, the payer recovers the funds after the deadline with no action from Konnex. There is no administrative function capable of moving escrowed funds (SlowMist, Authority Control: Passed). Upgrades are timelocked with public advance notice. Circle and Tether retain freeze and blacklist powers over their own tokens in response to sanctions or lawful orders; this applies to those assets globally and sits outside Konnex's control.

Q78. How is a subnet created?

exchange review, second round, 29 April 2026, testnet subnets

Konnex uses a Substrate-based subnet creation flow. Subnet registration, miner/validator participation, staking, and reward settlement are onchain; subnet-specific incentive logic (scoring, input/output schema) lives as published code that miners and validators run.

Subnet creator flow: hold the minimum KNX stake required for subnet registration; define the subnet manifest (name, workload class, input/output schema, scoring function spec, miner/validator requirements, and public code repository); submit the subnet-creation extrinsic transaction via the Konnex CLI or a wallet-signed transaction, which locks the registration stake and writes subnet metadata onchain; the network allocates a NETUID and emits a subnet-created event upon finalisation; the subnet owner enables emissions and publishes the miner/validator templates; miners and validators register against the NETUID by staking and running the published code.

Q80. How does the ReadyMonitor workload run on the testnet, step by step?

exchange review, second round, 29 April 2026, client flows

Commercial context. ReadyMonitor is a drone-based asset-inspection company. Their customers (utilities, insurers, industrial operators) need verifiable evidence that an autonomous inspection was actually performed to specification.

Flow on testnet. The operator connects to a physical or simulated drone on the hardware link page of the subnet app and connects a wallet. The operator issues a natural-language mission, for example "Proceed to the red-brick building, photograph the roof." The mission is wrapped as a task transaction and signed onchain. Multiple miners on the aerial subnet receive the task and, in parallel, produce candidate action sequences. Validators score candidates on safety, efficiency, task-match, and coherence; the top-scoring sequence is selected and executed. For safety, analytical heuristics are used alongside AI models to prevent collisions. Execution proceeds step by step with per-step re-scoring against the live sensor stream. On completion, validators issue a Proof-of-Physical-Work (PoPW) record: a signed attestation binding the original instruction hash, the pose/telemetry trace, captured media hashes, and per-step scores. PoPW is written onchain; the winning miner is rewarded; the operator receives an auditable inspection record.

The inspection record is a cryptographic, third-party-verifiable artefact, which is what ReadyMonitor's utility-company customers require to accept autonomous inspections for regulatory and insurance purposes.

Q81. How does the Exyn Technologies workload run on the testnet, step by step?

exchange review, second round, 29 April 2026, client flows

Commercial context. Exyn operates autonomous aerial systems for complex indoor and GPS-denied environments (mining, construction, industrial scanning). Their customers make engineering decisions on the 3D maps and inspection artefacts Exyn produces and require provenance for those artefacts. The Exyn workload has two parts, both served by the aerial subnet.

Part A, autonomous flight: mechanics identical to the ReadyMonitor flow.

Part B, 3D map generation / semantic SLAM. The user submits a sensor stream, either live from an Exyn drone or uploaded as a pre-recorded RGBD plus telemetry bundle; the submission is hashed and registered as a task transaction. Miners on the SLAM workload each run their reconstruction pipeline and produce a candidate 3D mesh with semantic labels. Validators score candidates on geometric coverage, mesh fidelity, semantic accuracy against held-out ground truth, and runtime efficiency. The best reconstruction is returned to the user; its hash is written onchain; the producing miner receives the subnet reward. PoPW attests that the returned map was derived from the submitted sensor stream and scored above threshold, which is the provenance Exyn's customers require.

Q82. How does the Veloce robot kitchen workload run on the testnet, step by step?

exchange review, second round, 29 April 2026, client flows

Commercial context. Veloce operates autonomous kitchen robots. At franchise scale, the operational requirement is a verifiable per-order record that the robot executed the customer's instruction faithfully, for quality control, food safety audit, and cross-operator reconciliation. Veloce has 8 pasta recipes, where different kitchen equipment, ingredients and timings are used; powering the robo-kitchen with VLA allows the chef to automate the work in human language.

Flow on testnet. Veloce's kitchen robot (physical arm in deployment; simulator on testnet) connects to the Konnex endpoint via the operator wallet. The operator or upstream order system issues a task prompt, for example "Take the basket from the table and put it into the microwave and power on for 2 minutes." The task is signed and broadcast as a subnet transaction. Miners on the manipulation subnet each run a VLA policy and produce candidate manipulation trajectories. Validators score candidates on safety (no collisions, stable grasps), task-match (correct object, correct action), and efficiency (frames/tokens used). The top-scoring trajectory is executed, with per-step re-scoring. On task success, PoPW is issued, attesting the robot executed the instructed task, not an adjacent one. A verifiable "the robot did what was ordered" record is the substrate for scaled operations across franchised locations, and for future reward-for-correct-execution economics between operators, customers, and insurers.

Q83. What is the role structure and how does Konnex differ from Bittensor?

exchange review, second round, 29 April 2026, architecture and differentiation

Konnex deliberately inherits the four-role economic structure (subnet creators, miners, validators, stakers) from Bittensor. The Phase 0 testnet implements the inherited subnet layer and the Escrow pallet smart contract for PoPW; the Konnex-specific primitives (stablecoin settlement, dual-staking, mesh transport) ship progressively through mainnet Phase 1 and 2.

Miner: Bittensor has a low TAO registration fee, compute-heavy, competing on model quality per subnet. Konnex testnet: low testKNX registration, compute-heavy, same structure; models are VLA/SLAM/navigation rather than generic inference. Mainnet target: a stablecoin bond option for higher-assurance commercial jobs and composite bids (motion and perception).

Validator: Bittensor is stake-weight gated (top-N by stake) with Yuma Consensus aggregating validator scores and bonds accumulating in backed miners. Konnex testnet: stake-weight gated with KNX staking; scoring aggregation follows the Yuma model inherited from Substrate/Subtensor; validators score on PoPW-relevant KPIs (safety, task-match, efficiency). Mainnet target: dual stake, KNX for protocol security and stablecoins for settlement assurance, the assurance stake compensating counterparties for settlement-layer faults.

Subnet creator: Bittensor locks a TAO bond for a subnet slot, defines the incentive mechanism offchain and earns an emission share. Konnex testnet: same structure with a KNX bond; three subnets instantiated by the core team, aligned to the signed commercial workload classes (Exyn, Veloce, ReadyMonitor). Mainnet target: open subnet creation via a public manifest standard, so commercial partners can publish workload-specific subnets directly.

Staker / delegator: Bittensor stakes TAO behind validators and earns a share of validator emissions. Konnex testnet: same structure with testKNX. Mainnet target: third-party stakers can additionally supply stablecoin bonds to robotic bond pools (BondMatrix), earning stablecoin-denominated yield priced by reputation.

What Konnex adds. Workload class: Bittensor is optimised for free-form AI inference where outputs are tokens; Konnex is purpose-built for embodied and physical-world workloads (VLA, SLAM, navigation, manipulation) where outputs are physical actions or artefacts with real-world safety and provenance requirements. Robotic identity and inter-robotic smart contracts: each robot possesses a cryptographically proven identity and the built-in capability to execute PoPW contracts directly with one another. Verification primitive: validator scoring produces a PoPW record binding the task instruction, policy trace, sensor observations and per-step scores into a single verifiable onchain artefact; PoPW is the settlement trigger. Stablecoin-native settlement (mainnet): escrow, payouts and penalties settle in stablecoins; KNX handles security, governance and fees with an auto-buyback flywheel from a small stablecoin flow fee. Dual-staked validators (mainnet): a KNX security stake (slashed for protocol/consensus misbehaviour) and a stablecoin assurance stake (compensates counterparties for settlement-layer faults such as wrongful PoPW approval), both scaling with onchain trust.
